In the soot-stained town of Blackwood, nestled amidst the chugging engines and clanking machinery of the burgeoning Industrial Revolution, there lived a man named Silas Piddleworth. He was a mender of boots and shoes, a humble cobler, with a face as long as a damp Wednesday. His shop, tucked between a chandlery and a blacksmithy, was always bustling, for everyone from mill workers to mine drills wore out their shoes rapidly in Blackwood. However, Silas was not known for his fine stitching alone. He was also known for his unusual humor which many people found peculiar but endearing. One day, a young woman named Evelyn, with sparkling eyes and a spirit to match, swept into Silas’s shop. She was not there for a mending. Instead, she was seeking employment and she had a proposition. Her idea was to brighten the workers with laughter, by performing in between shifts to distract from the monotony of factory work and the drudgery of mining. With a laugh that was infectious and an endless stream of jokes and witticisms, she convinced him that laughter might sell more shoes, as happy workers needed a pair more frequently, or so her theory went. Silas was skeptical but the people of Blackwood had been wearing shoes like they were going out of fashion so he saw no harm in Evelyn’s idea. She performed the first of her comedy routines on a small stage in the town square during the lunch hour the following Saturday. To his astonishment, and delight, her jokes about factory accidents, misplaced mine canary and misbehaved steam engine started the whole town laughing until the day grew dark and the workers reluctantly left her stage.
