In the grimy, smog-laden city of Manchester, where the Industrial Revolution had taken root like a festering wound, there existed a peculiar establishment known as The Laughing Factory. It was not a place of merriment, but rather a grimy tavern where laughter echoed as falsely as the clanking of machinery that dominated the city's heartbeat. The Laughing Factory was owned by one Ebenezer Scruggs, a man as humorless as his name suggested. His face was a landscape of harsh lines, his eyes small and shrewd, always calculating. He was not a man to be trifled with, yet he was respected, for he had a peculiar gift: he could predict the future with uncanny accuracy. This gift had made him a wealthy man, but it had also made him a lonely one. The tavern's patrons were a motley crew of factory workers, dockhands, and the occasional down-on-their-luck gentleman. They would gather each evening, seeking solace in the bottom of their glasses, their laughter as forced as the smiles of the painted ladies who plied their trade in the shadows. The only genuine laughter came from a young woman named Eliza, who worked at the nearby cotton mill. She was a tiny thing, with a face like a sunflower and eyes that sparkled with life. Her laughter was infectious, a rare melody in the grim symphony of the city. One evening, as the tavern filled with the usual din of forced mirth and clinking glasses, Scruggs stood behind the bar, his gaze distant, his mind elsewhere. He was thinking about the future, as he often did. He had seen a vision, a terrible one, of a great fire that would consume the city, a fire started by a careless spark in a cotton mill. He had seen the flames licking at the buildings, heard the screams of the people, felt the heat of the inferno.
